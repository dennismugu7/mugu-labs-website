Every year it's the same story. December arrives with its fares, food, gatherings and gifts, and we spend like the month has no end. Then January turns up with school fees, rent and an empty wallet, and feels about ninety days long.

There's a reason we call it *Njaanuary*.

The fix isn't spending nothing in December. It's deciding now, while it's still October, what December is allowed to cost, and quietly putting that money aside before the season starts.

## Why October is the right time

Three things pile up this year:

- **The long holiday starts early.** Schools close for the year on 23 October and reopen on Monday 4 January 2027. That's about ten weeks of children at home: more food, more outings, more "Mum, can I…".
- **Prices are climbing.** Inflation reached 6.8% in September, the highest in a year, with food up 9.5% and transport up 15.6% compared with a year ago.
- **January comes straight after.** Term-one fees, uniforms and supplies arrive just as the Christmas spending ends.

From now until schools reopen is roughly **twelve weeks**. That's twelve chances to set a little aside, instead of one painful month.

## Step 1: List your big five

Most festive budgets come down to five buckets. Write a rough number next to each. Don't aim for perfect; last year's spending is a good starting guess.

1. **Travel**: going upcountry, or hosting people who come to you.
2. **Food and gatherings**: Christmas lunch, visitors, contributions to family events.
3. **Gifts and family support**: presents, and the money relatives will ask for.
4. **Kids at home**: snacks, outings and activities across the long holiday.
5. **January**: fees, uniforms, books and rent. This one is not optional.

## Step 2: Divide by twelve

Turn each total into a weekly amount. Here's an example. The numbers are only illustrations, so swap in your own.

| Bucket | Estimate (KES) | Per week (÷12) |
|---|---|---|
| Travel | 6,000 | 500 |
| Food and gatherings | 9,000 | 750 |
| Gifts and family support | 6,000 | 500 |
| Kids at home | 4,800 | 400 |
| January fees and supplies | 24,000 | 2,000 |
| **Total** | **49,800** | **4,150** |

Paid monthly? Divide by three instead: in this example, that's 16,600 from each of your October, November and December salaries.

If the weekly number looks scary, that's useful information. It's far better to see it now and trim the plan than to discover it on 2 January.

## Step 3: Pay January first

When you trim, protect January. Christmas can be smaller and still be lovely. School fees can't be smaller. Set aside the January bucket first each week, then fill the others with what's left.

## Step 4: Move the money somewhere you won't touch

Money sitting in your everyday account tends to get spent. Put the weekly amount somewhere separate the same day it arrives: a savings account, a locked savings option, or a chama contribution with a December payout. Out of sight really does help.

## Step 5: Decide your "no" early

The hardest festive spending isn't in shops. It's the requests. Have the conversations now, kindly:

- Agree a gift limit with your family, or a secret-Santa style draw instead of gifts for everyone.
- Decide how much you'll contribute to gatherings, and offer it early so it feels generous, not reluctant.
- If you're helping relatives, pick a number you can sustain and stick to it.

A plan written in October makes "I've already set aside what I can" easy to say in December.

## Your one-page checklist

- [ ] Write a rough number for each of the big five
- [ ] Divide by twelve (or by three salaries)
- [ ] Set aside the January amount first, every week
- [ ] Move the money out of your everyday account
- [ ] Talk to family about gifts and contributions
- [ ] Check in once a month and adjust
