There's a quiet gap in how a lot of software gets made. It's designed and tested on the newest phones, over fast office Wi-Fi, by people who never think about how much data a screen costs. Then it's handed to someone with a three-year-old handset, half a bundle left and a battery that's seen better days.

We'd rather build for that second person. Here's why, and what it means in practice.

## Who's actually holding the phone

In Kenya, Android is close to everything: StatCounter put its share of mobile phones at about 95% in September 2026. And the country is still moving onto smartphones. The Communications Authority counted 52.3 million smartphones and 27.4 million feature phones connected by June 2026.

Put those together and you get a simple picture. Many of the people we build for are on budget Android phones, often second-hand or a few years old, with limited storage, on prepaid data bundles. Many are using a smartphone app for running their business or their money for the first time.

If an app doesn't work well for them, it doesn't work.

## What "built to be left alone" means to us

One of our principles is that our apps should quietly do their job, even on a five-year-old Android. In practice, that shapes a lot of small decisions.

**Respect the data bundle.**
Every image, animation and background refresh costs someone money. We keep screens light, avoid loading things nobody asked for, and think twice before adding anything that runs in the background.

**Respect the storage.**
When a phone is full, the first apps to go are the big ones. A small app is more likely to stay installed.

**Respect the battery.**
An app that drains the battery gets uninstalled, however useful it is. We try hard not to keep the phone awake or busy when you're not using it.

**Assume the network will drop.**
Signal comes and goes, in a matatu, in a basement salon, upcountry. We design so that a slow or dropped connection is an inconvenience, not a disaster, and so you're never left wondering whether something saved.

**Make it readable and tappable.**
Bigger text, clear contrast and buttons you can hit with your thumb on a small screen, in bright sunlight. These help everyone, not only people with older phones.

**Fewer screens, fewer settings.**
Every extra option is something to learn and something that can go wrong. If a feature isn't pulling its weight, it goes.

## Why it's worth the extra effort

Designing for older phones is harder. You can't hide a slow screen behind a fast processor. But the result is an app that feels quick and calm on *every* phone, including the new ones. Building for the edge makes the middle better.

And it's simply fair. The people who most need tools that save them time and money are often the ones with the least patience left for apps that waste either.
